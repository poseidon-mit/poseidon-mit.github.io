# Certificate Award Ceremony Speech — MIT CTO Cohort 2026

---

Twelve months ago, I sat in a room very much like this one. [PAUSE]

I looked around and I did not know a single face. Eighty people. More than a hundred and fifty countries. Every one of us had spent years leading technology teams. And every one of us had chosen to come here because we felt the same thing: that what got us here would not be enough for what comes next.

That first residential week, Dr. Nino asked us a question. He said: "What is your Leadership Signature?" [PAUSE] I remember the silence. Not because we did not have answers. Because we realized our old answers no longer fit.

That discomfort — that honest moment of not knowing — was where the real work began.

[PAUSE]

Over twelve months, Professor de Weck taught us to see technology not as products, but as systems that evolve. Professor Shah showed us how to let data speak before our assumptions do. Dr. Sanchez and Professor Williams did not just teach us about generative AI. They put it in our hands and said: now build something real.

And we did.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want to carry out of this room today. One idea. Not a framework. A pattern.

Steam did not just power machines. It rebuilt cities. Electricity did not just light rooms. It forced factories to redesign from the floor up. The technology was necessary. But the real value came from the people who figured out how to reorganize around it. [PAUSE]

AI is no different.

Our team built Poseidon — a working platform with a functional site, graphics, and video — in three weeks. That work used to take departments and months. But the AI was not the reason it worked. We were. We learned to hand off tasks to agents, to trust each other across time zones, to let go of the instinct to control every step. The technology was the engine. The reorganization was the breakthrough.

That is the lesson. Not what AI can do. What we can become when we learn to work beside it.

[PAUSE]

MIT was founded on a motto: Mens et Manus. Mind and Hand. Know the science. Apply it. [PAUSE]

This program added a third word. Cor. Heart.

Because every system we build has a human on the other end. Every algorithm we deploy reshapes someone's work, someone's livelihood, someone's day. The technology leaders this world needs are not the ones who move fastest. They are the ones who pause long enough to ask: who does this serve?

[PAUSE]

Dr. Nino, Dr. Sanchez, Professor Shah, Professor Williams, Professor de Weck — thank you. You did not give us answers. You gave us better questions. That is a far harder thing to teach.

[PAUSE — turn to the cohort]

And to all of you. We came here as strangers from every corner of this planet. We leave as colleagues who have argued over roadmaps at midnight, pitched ideas we were not sure would work, and learned to trust people we had never met in person.

That trust is the most valuable thing I take home. [PAUSE]

Let us go use it well.

## Delivery Notes

- **Opening pace**: Start slowly and quietly. The first three sentences set the room. Do not rush. Let "I did not know a single face" land.
- **Faculty acknowledgment (middle section)**: When naming each professor, look directly at them. Brief nod to each. This is a thank-you, not a roll call — keep it warm and flowing.
- **"Steam did not just power machines" passage**: This is the intellectual center. Slow your pace here. Pause after "AI is no different." Let the audience complete the thought themselves.
- **Poseidon section**: Shift to a slightly more conversational, energized tone. You lived this — let that show. But stay measured. The restraint is what makes it credible.
- **"Cor. Heart." moment**: Pause after "Cor." Let the single word sit. No hand-over-heart gesture needed — the word does the work if you give it silence.
- **Final turn to the cohort**: Physically turn your body toward your peers. Drop your voice slightly for "That trust is the most valuable thing I take home." This is not a declaration. It is a private truth said in a public room.
- **Closing line**: "Let us go use it well." — Deliver this simply, at normal volume, with a small nod. Then stop. Do not add "thank you" or "congratulations" after it. The line is the ending.
