## Constraints

### Tone and Style

- **Conversational authority.** Speak as a peer reflecting on a shared experience, not as a lecturer presenting findings. The room already knows the frameworks. They need to feel them.
- **Plain language.** If a sentence requires a second read to understand, rewrite it. No jargon unless it is a term the cohort lived through together (listed above). Use those shared-experience terms as emotional anchors, not as definitions.
- **Short sentences.** Average sentence length under 15 words. Mix rhythm: a long sentence followed by a short punch. Let silence do work.
- **No academic hedging.** Replace "it is imperative that we consider" with "we must." Replace "this paradigm represents" with "this changes how we."
- **No hype words.** Ban: unprecedented, paradigm shift, cutting-edge, exponential era, precipice, cognitive frontier, strategic business paradigm shift, game-changer, disruptive. These words have been emptied of meaning by overuse. Find fresher, more specific ways to say what you mean.
- **Warm but not sentimental.** The closing should move people without manipulating them. Earn the emotion through the story, not through adjectives.
- **Non-native English speakers.** Many participants speak English as a second or third language. Prefer simple vocabulary. Avoid complex subordinate clauses. Favor active voice.

### Structure (3-beat arc)

**Beat 1: The Shared Starting Point (~90 seconds)**
- Open with a specific, relatable moment from the program. Not a grand statement about the state of the world. A moment. Possibilities: the first Residential Week when strangers from 150 countries sat together for the first time. The late-night video calls across time zones to finish an assignment. A specific classroom moment when something clicked. The nervousness before an Impact Project pitch. Ground the audience in a memory they share.
- Transition naturally into what brought everyone here: the recognition that leading technology in 2026 requires something different than it did even five years ago. State this simply. One sentence. Then move on.

**Beat 2: The Lesson (~60 seconds)**
- Deliver one core insight from the program. Not three frameworks. One idea, stated clearly, supported by one concrete example.
- The core insight to convey (restate in plain language): **The biggest breakthroughs in history never came from the technology itself. They came from people who figured out how to reorganize around it.** Steam did not just power machines; it rebuilt cities. Electricity did not just light rooms; it redesigned factories from the ground up. AI will not just automate tasks; it will reshape how entire organizations think and move.
- Anchor this with the Poseidon Impact Project as proof. In three weeks, a small team used AI workflows to build what used to take departments and months. Not because the AI was magic. Because the team learned to work differently. That is the real lesson.
- Keep the Poseidon reference brief and concrete. Do not over-explain it. Everyone in the room lived it or saw it pitched.

**Beat 3: The Charge (~60 seconds)**
- Transition from "what we learned" to "what we carry forward."
- Weave in the MIT motto evolution: Mens et Manus (Mind and Hand) is the Institute's foundation. But this program added something: Cor (Heart). We leave here as technologists who also lead with empathy, who build teams where people feel safe enough to experiment, who remember that every system we design has a human on the other end.
- Close with a direct, simple call: acknowledge the faculty (they are sitting right there), thank them, then turn to the cohort. End on connection, not grandeur. The last line should feel like something one colleague says to another at the end of a long, hard, worthwhile year together.

### Content Requirements

- **Must include:** Acknowledgment of MIT faculty by name (listed above). Reference to the Impact Project. Reference to Mens, Manus et Cor. A forward-looking charge to the cohort.
- **Must avoid:** Defining frameworks the audience already knows (do not explain what sensemaking is, what distributed leadership is, what the 4-CAP+ model is, what a Leadership Signature is). Listing multiple frameworks. Quoting statistics or data points (this is not a keynote; it is a personal reflection). Using the phrase "100x productivity gain" literally (show it through the Poseidon example, do not label it). Mentioning the program cost or logistics.
- **Cultural sensitivity:** The audience spans 150+ countries. Avoid idioms, sports metaphors, or cultural references that do not travel globally. Use universal human experiences: learning something hard, working with strangers who became collaborators, the discomfort of not knowing, the satisfaction of building something together. Remember many participants are non-native English speakers.

### Delivery Notes (include as a brief appendix)

- Where to pause. Where to slow down. Where to make eye contact with specific groups (faculty, then cohort).
- One physical gesture suggestion maximum. Less is more.
- The speech should be written to sound natural when read aloud by a non-native English speaker. Test every sentence by asking: "Would a real person say this out loud to a room of peers, clearly and without stumbling?"

## What "Award-Winning" Means Here

The selection panel will compare drafts from two nominees per program. They are looking for:

1. **Authenticity.** Does this sound like a real person reflecting honestly, or like a corporate keynote?
2. **Universality.** Will a CTO from Lagos and a CTO from Seoul and a CTO from Sao Paulo all feel seen?
3. **Memorability.** Will the audience remember one line a week later? Write that line.
4. **Brevity.** Respect the 3-minute constraint. Every sentence must earn its place.
5. **Resonance as professional guideline.** The audience should leave with one clear idea they can carry into their daily work. Not motivation. Direction.

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- Opening with "We stand at the precipice of..." or any variation of "we live in unprecedented times."
- Listing what AI can do. Everyone knows.
- Name-dropping frameworks as proof of learning. Show the learning through story.
- Ending with "Together, we will build a future worthy of..." This is a cliche. Find a real ending.
- Using the word "journey" more than once (the program materials already overuse it).
- Stacking superlatives: "the most remarkable, most brilliant, most extraordinary cohort."
- Any sentence that could appear in a LinkedIn post without modification.
- Treating the speech like a TED talk keynote. This is a peer address at a graduation ceremony. Intimate, not performative.
