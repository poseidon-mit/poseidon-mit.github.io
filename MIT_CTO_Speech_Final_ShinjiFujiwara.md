# MIT CTO Certificate Award Ceremony — Speech Draft

**Speaker:** Shinji Fujiwara (United States)
**Program:** Chief Technology Officer
**Coordinator:** Claudio Aguilera
**Duration:** ~3 minutes (~440 words)

---

Eleven months ago, I sat in a room very much like this one for our first residential week. I looked around and saw almost no familiar faces. About a hundred people from dozens of countries. We were tired from the flights and a little nervous.

I joined the CTO program for a simple reason: what had brought me here would not be enough for what comes next. Looking around today, I believe everyone in this room felt the same way.

In that first week, Dr. Westerman said something I have not forgotten: "Technology evolves exponentially, but organizations change much more slowly." The room went silent. Not because we didn't understand. Because we recognized ourselves in it. That moment was a turning point.

*(pause)*

Over the months that followed, our professors expanded how we think. Professor de Weck taught us to see technology not as products, but as systems that evolve. Professor Shah showed us how to let data speak before our assumptions do. Dr. Sanchez and Professor Williams didn't just teach us about generative AI. They gave us frameworks to evaluate it — and then told us: now go build something real.

Here is what I want to carry out of this room today.

The steam engine didn't just power machines. It rebuilt cities. Electricity didn't just light rooms. It forced entire industries to reinvent how people worked. The technology was necessary. But the real value came from the leaders who figured out how to reorganize people around it.

AI is the same.

Our team built Poseidon — a working AI platform with a functional website, a product video, and a full business case — in just three weeks. That scope used to require dozens of people and months of work. AI helped, but *we* did it. We moved faster. We made decisions faster. We let go of controlling every single step, but we stayed at the center. This is how people will work with AI going forward.

That is the lesson. Not what AI can do. What *we* can do with it.

And yet — every system we build has a human on the other end. Every algorithm we deploy reshapes someone's work, someone's livelihood, someone's day. The technology leaders this world needs are not the ones who move fastest. They are the ones who pause long enough to ask: *who does this serve?*

*(pause)*

Dr. Nino, Professor de Weck, Professor Shah, Professor Williams, Dr. Abel — thank you. You did not give us answers. You gave us better questions. And to Claudio Aguilera, the learning facilitators, and the Global Alumni team — we could not have completed this program without you.

To our cohort: we came here as strangers from every corner of this planet. We leave with sharper minds, steadier judgment, and a wider vision.

My experience at MIT has been deeply precious to me, and I will carry what I learned here for the rest of my life.

Today we leave MIT. But this is not an ending. It is the start — of leading technology in a way that earns trust.

Thank you.

---

## Delivery Notes

1. **Two key pauses:** After "turning point" and after "who does this serve?" — these are your strongest emotional beats. Let them breathe for 2-3 seconds.
2. **Slow down on Poseidon:** This is your proof point and what makes the speech *yours*. Let the audience picture it.
3. **Italicize with your voice:** When you say "we did it" and "we can do with it," lean into the word "we" slightly. That emphasis carries the thesis.
4. **Final line:** "Earns trust" should land quietly and firmly, not loudly. Understated confidence.
